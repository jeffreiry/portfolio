# Case Study Writing Guide
## How to write like a human, not like a template

Derived from a direct comparison between an AI-polished case (85% AI score) and a 
human-rewritten version of the same project. The differences below are what moved 
the needle.

---

## The core problem with AI-sounding cases

AI doesn't lie. It just removes everything uncomfortable — doubt, friction, failure, 
personality. What's left is technically correct and completely unbelievable. 
Recruiters who read dozens of portfolios recognize the pattern immediately, even 
if they can't name it.

The fix isn't making the writing worse. It's making it *yours*.

---

## 1. Lead with what surprised you, not what you did

**AI version:**
> "Discovery revealed two linked problems, not one."

**Human version:**
> "The Clarity recordings were the turning point. We could see exactly where people 
> stopped: read the answer, then opened a new tab. Every time."

The AI version announces a finding. The human version shows the moment you found it.
Start sections with what changed your understanding, not with what you set out to do.

---

## 2. Name your doubt

Every project has a decision you were least certain about. Say which one.

**AI version:** (no doubt expressed anywhere in 900 words)

**Human version:**
> "This was the decision I was least certain about early on."

One sentence. It signals self-awareness, intellectual honesty, and — crucially — 
that a real person made this decision under real uncertainty. Templates don't have 
doubt. You do.

---

## 3. Break structural symmetry

If every section follows the same pattern, it reads as generated. The Problem → 
Options → Choice → Why framework is useful — but applied identically four times 
in a row, it becomes a fingerprint.

**Fix:** vary the structure between decisions.
- One decision as narrative ("we almost went the other way")
- One decision with the reasoning first, the choice second
- One decision that acknowledges what you couldn't validate

Same content, different shape. Detectors read rhythm, not just words.

---

## 4. Use short sentences after long ones

AI tends toward uniform sentence length. Human writing bursts — long clause, 
then a short one that lands.

**AI version:**
> "Without traceability, a right answer and a wrong answer look identical — and 
> in a compliance context, that's enough to stall adoption."

**Human version:**
> "A correct answer and a wrong answer looked identical. In a compliance context, 
> that's enough to kill adoption entirely."

Same meaning. The period after "identical" changes the feel completely.

---

## 5. Replace "non-negotiable" sentences with specific costs

Generic assertions of importance are an AI tell. Concrete consequences are not.

**AI version:**
> "Trust is non-negotiable in enterprise."

**Human version:**
> "Wrong answers have real cost here — incorrect decision, compliance risk. 
> An answer without a visible source isn't just incomplete. It's unusable."

The rule: never say something "matters" or is "critical". Say what breaks if it fails.

---

## 6. Mention what you couldn't validate

AI-generated cases are always confident. Real projects have gaps.

**AI version:** ships a decision, moves on

**Human version:**
> "Six months to design and ship a company-wide platform — without being able to 
> validate all hypotheses with real users before launch — is genuinely tight. 
> We inferred a lot from market benchmarks. Most of it held up."

"Most of it held up" is doing enormous work here. It says: I know the difference 
between what I proved and what I bet on.

---

## 7. Replace the Impact placeholder or remove it entirely

An Impact section with no numbers is worse than no Impact section. It signals 
that the data exists but you're hiding it — which reads as either dishonesty 
or carelessness.

**If you have data:** use it, even as relative proxies ("3× session length vs MVP").  
**If you don't:** replace the section entirely with "What I'd Do Differently" 
or fold the learnings together.

A section that ends with "feedback collected via CSAT mechanisms" tells the 
reader nothing and costs you credibility.

---

## 8. End personally, not conclusively

AI endings summarize. Human endings land.

**AI version:**
> "Visible traceability isn't a UX detail; it's the condition for a correct 
> answer to actually be used."

**Human version:**
> "That's the thing I'll carry from this project."

The insight can be the same. The last sentence should feel like you closed 
the laptop and thought about it on the way home.

---

## 9. Mention firsts, context, and what shaped your approach

AI doesn't have professional history. You do. A first project of a type, 
a constraint that changed how you worked, a context that made this project 
different — these are details that are impossible to fabricate.

> "This was also my first project designing a conversational AI interface. 
> That matters for how I approached it: I had no prior instinct to fall back on, 
> so I leaned harder on benchmarking and on what the recordings were showing me."

This isn't vulnerable — it's specific. Specificity is the opposite of AI.

---

## 10. The final test before publishing

Read the case out loud. Stop at every sentence that you wouldn't say to a 
colleague over coffee. Rewrite those sentences first.

If you'd say "we shipped a direction I believed in, but belief isn't the same 
as evidence" — keep it.  
If you wouldn't say "the experience had to make the source of information 
non-optional" — rewrite it.

Your spoken voice is your calibration tool.

---

## 11. Watch the em-dash

The em-dash (`—`) is one of the most consistent markers of AI-generated text. The pattern detectors recognize:

> "Main clause — expansion or qualification."

AI uses this structure constantly because it connects two ideas without needing a real transition. The problem isn't the dash itself. It's the frequency and the shape: always the same direction, always expanding, never interrupting or contradicting.

**The AI pattern:**
> "accurate corporate knowledge, but inaccessible — and an MVP that answered questions but wasn't trusted"
> "a right answer and a wrong answer look identical — and in a compliance context, that's enough to stall adoption"

Two em-dashes in two sentences, both following the same rhythm: statement, dash, consequence.

**The fix:** keep em-dashes, but use them less and vary what comes after. Humans also use dashes to interrupt, to change direction mid-thought, or to add something that slightly contradicts what came before. If you have more than two em-dashes per section, at least one should become a period.

**Practical rule:** after writing a section, search for ` — ` and read each one out loud. If it sounds like a bullet point that got attached to the previous sentence, replace it with a period and a new sentence.

---

## Quick reference — swaps that lower AI score

| AI phrase | Human swap |
|-----------|------------|
| "non-negotiable" | name the specific cost if it fails |
| "revealed two linked problems" | "the recordings showed exactly where people stopped" |
| "reduces blank-screen anxiety" | "people don't know where to start — the prompts show them" |
| "the experience had to..." | "we needed to..." or "I wanted to..." |
| "collected via CSAT mechanisms" | remove entirely or replace with What I'd Do Differently |
| four identical Problem→Options→Choice→Why | vary structure between decisions |
| no doubt anywhere | name the one decision you were least certain about |
| "that's enough to stall adoption" | "that's enough to kill it" — shorter, sharper |

## What to extract from yourself before writing

These questions produce the sentences detectors can't flag:

1. What was the moment you had the most doubt?
2. What did you get wrong before you got it right?
3. What's one thing you would validate differently if you did it again?
4. Was there a specific user quote, recording, or metric that changed your mind?
5. What made this project different from others you've done?

The answers don't need to be long. One sentence each is enough. 
They're the skeleton the rest of the case hangs on.
